*Washington has written two numbers next to India's name, and the space between them is the most interesting part of this week's trade news.*

On Wednesday, November 25, the office of the US Trade Representative (USTR), America's trade negotiator, published a notice about India. It proposes a tariff of **up to 25%** on Indian goods. In the same breath, it says the rate could be **17%** if India signs what it calls an "Agreement on Reciprocal Trade" with the United States.

A tariff is a tax charged at the border on goods coming in from another country. The American company importing the goods pays it, and usually passes part of it on to shoppers or squeezes the seller for a lower price.

So the US is offering India a choice with a price tag attached. Sign a deal, pay 17. Do not, pay 25. Everything in this story comes back to that gap of **8 percentage points**.

## Where the two numbers come from

The proposal is the end of an investigation the USTR opened on **March 11** into 16 economies, under a 1974 American law called Section 301. It lets the US punish trade practices it considers unfair. The question was "structural excess capacity", meaning factories built with government help that produce far more than the home market needs, with the surplus pushed abroad at low prices.

On November 25, the USTR found against 14 of the 16, India among them. Mexico and Norway got no finding for now. According to the [notice itself](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/Press/Releases/2026/India%20FRN.pdf), the 25% would apply "net of existing Section 301 tariffs". Trade lawyers at [Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/trade-and-manufacturing-monitor/ustr-proposes-section-301-overcapacity-tariffs-on-14-of-16-economies-investigated-public-comment-period-and-public-hearing-will-precede-any-final-action) read that to mean the new rate would not be stacked on top of the 10% forced labour tariff India already pays. In other words, 25% would be the total, not 35%.

That 10% has been in place since **July 24**, after a separate Section 301 case about forced labour, as [Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/economy-and-policy/why-us-10-pc-forced-labour-tariffs-on-india-matter-bilateral-trade-agreement-ustr-section-301-donald-trump) and [Upstox](https://upstox.com/news/business-news/latest-updates/india-placed-in-lower-10-tariff-bracket-in-us-section-301-forced-labour-action/article-197484/) reported at the time. It replaced a temporary 10% global tariff the White House brought in after the US Supreme Court struck down its earlier tariffs in February, per [News On AIR](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/white-house-says-india-to-face-temporary-10-per-cent-tariff-after-new-us-order).

```stats
10% | Extra US tariff most Indian goods pay today
17% | Proposed rate if India signs a trade deal
25% | Proposed rate if it does not
```

## What 8 points looks like on a shirt

Numbers like these are easy to nod at and hard to feel, so try one shipment. Say a Tiruppur factory sells a US store a box of T-shirts worth **$100**. At 17%, the importer pays $17 at the American border. At 25%, it pays $25. That is our own simple arithmetic, ignoring the regular duty that garments already pay.

Eight dollars on every hundred does not sound like much until you remember who else is selling T-shirts. The same notices give rivals their own pairs of numbers.

```bars
What each rival could pay, with and without a deal
Proposed Section 301 rate, percent (net of existing forced labour tariffs)
Vietnam, no deal | 35 | 35%
India, no deal | 25 | 25%
Vietnam, with deal | 20 | 20%
Singapore, no deal | 20 | 20%
Bangladesh | 18 | 18%
India, with deal | 17 | 17%
EU and Japan | 15 | 15%
Source: USTR proposals of November 25, 2026, compiled by Kelley Drye
```

Without a deal, India would pay more than Bangladesh, a big rival in clothing. With one, it slips just under. That small swing is the whole design: the lower rate is only on offer if India signs, which makes saying no expensive.

## Who is in the line of fire, and who is not

The proposal does not hit everything. The notice exempts goods already covered by other American tariffs, such as steel, aluminium, copper, cars and car parts. It also carries a long annex of items left out. We checked that list: **smartphones**, most **medicines** in finished form and **refined fuels** like diesel and petrol are on it. Solar modules, garments, cut diamonds and jewellery are not.

GTRI estimates that about **40%** of India's exports to the US would stay outside the new duty, led by those phones, medicines and fuels. Around **50 to 52%** would be hit, mostly labour heavy goods such as textiles, leather, garments and handicrafts, per Srivastava's comments to ANI carried by [The Tribune](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/ajay-srivastava/us-questions-indias-pli-make-in-india-schemes-while-defending-its-own-manufacturing-interests-by-tariffs-gtri). For these exporters, the 8 points are the whole story. The US is India's largest export market, taking about **$50 billion** of goods in the first seven months of this financial year, according to [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/diplomacy/india-takes-note-of-us-new-proposed-tariffs-says-it-has-no-excess-capacity-in-any-sector/3109418/).

If you want the longer story of how India's export push got here, we covered the [gap between its $1 trillion target and reality](/explainers/india-promised-the-world-1-trillion-in-exports-this-year-it-fell-150-billion-short) in August.

## What the US says India did wrong

The USTR's case is broad. It names Make in India, production linked incentive (PLI) schemes, which pay companies for making more at home, and Atmanirbhar Bharat. It points to steel, solar, health and electronics. It says India's solar module capacity has reached roughly **140 to 160 gigawatts**, around three times domestic demand of 40 to 50 gigawatts. It also lists weak labour protections and "financial repression", meaning policies that keep interest rates low or steer loans to favoured industries.

## What India says back

India's commerce ministry said it "has taken note" of the notice and is examining it. Its central line is blunt: "India does not have structural excess capacity in any sector." It wants these concerns settled inside the trade talks, not through tariffs.

Srivastava went further, calling excess capacity "just a pretence for an extractive one-sided trade deal", as reported by [Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/business/trade-deal-or-tariff-pressure-gtri-questions-washingtons-motives-behind-proposed-duties). He suggested India point to America's own farm subsidies in its reply.

## How long India has to decide

Not long, but not this week either. Written comments are due on **January 5, 2027**, and public hearings begin in Washington on **January 25**. USTR chief Jamieson Greer told CNBC he did not expect any of the new duties to take effect "earlier than a couple months from now", per [IANS via India West](https://indiawest.com/greer-says-proposed-us-tariffs-on-india-unlikely-to-take-effect-for-months/). He also said that once existing tariffs are counted, "it really will just top up a few percentage points". How that applies to India's case has not been spelled out.

India, meanwhile, sounds keen to close. On Friday, Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal said "the US trade deal is close to culmination," [Reuters reported](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/india-us-trade-deal-nearing-052632979.html). That comes just weeks before India is expected to sign its [long negotiated deal with the European Union](/explainers/india-just-struck-the-biggest-trade-deal-in-its-history-it-could-not-have-come-at-a-better-time) in Brussels on December 16, [PTI reported](https://www.outlookindia.com/business/india-eu-trade-pact-likely-to-be-signed-on-december-16), citing an official. For background on how US politics feeds into all this, see our post on [what the US midterms mean for India](/blog/us-midterms-2026-what-it-means-for-india).

The 8 points are, in effect, an opening offer. What India gives up to collect them is the part nobody has published yet.
