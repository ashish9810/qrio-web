**The short answer:** a healthy news diet is small, varied and regular. You check at set times, rely on a few trusted sources, mix local, national and global stories, and choose more explanation than alarm.

It is not about eating less news for the sake of it. It is about choosing what goes on your plate, instead of finishing whatever the feed puts in front of you. Here is what research says, what a balanced plate looks like, and a sample week you can copy.

## What is a news diet?

Your news diet is everything you take in about the world. That includes which sources you use, how often you check, the formats you prefer and how much time it all takes.

The food comparison is useful because it moves the question away from "news good" or "news bad". Nobody asks whether food is bad for you. They ask what kind, how much and when. Library and media groups use the same idea. A [guide from the University of the District of Columbia](https://udc.libguides.com/blog/Creating-Your-News-Diet) suggests one international, one national, one local and one hyperlocal source. [News Co/Lab](https://newscollab.org/2018/12/24/you-are-what-you-read-why-a-balanced-information-diet-matters/) warns that leaning on one outlet or platform makes for a poor diet.

## What does research say about too much news?

The research points one way, but it comes with an important caveat. Most of it is correlational. It shows that heavy news use and stress go together, not that one causes the other.

Two studies are worth knowing.

**After the Boston Marathon bombings.** In a [2013 study in PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3890785), Holman, Garfin and Silver surveyed 4,675 American adults two to four weeks after the 2013 attack. People who reported six or more hours a day of bombing-related media in the week after were far more likely to report high acute stress than those with little exposure. In their models, heavy media exposure was linked to more stress than being at or near the bombing itself. The authors say plainly that their correlational study cannot prove cause, and that the link may run both ways: people who are already distressed may watch more.

**Problematic news habits.** In a [2022 study in Health Communication](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/08/220824102936.htm), McLaughlin, Gotlieb and Mills surveyed 1,100 US adults. They classed 16.5% as having severely problematic news consumption: feeling absorbed by the news, thinking about it constantly, struggling to stop and finding it got in the way of daily life. Of that group, 73.6% reported mental ill-being "quite a bit" or "very much", compared with 8% of everyone else. For physical complaints such as fatigue and poor concentration, the figures were 61% and 6.1%. The data came from one point in time, so again this shows an association, not proof.

The honest takeaway is not "news makes you ill". It is that very heavy, compulsive news use and feeling unwell often travel together. For a wider look at the evidence on scrolling, see [is doomscrolling bad for you?](/blog/is-doomscrolling-bad-for-you).

## Is too little news a problem too?

It can be. One of the researchers behind the 2022 study, Melissa Gotlieb, put it simply in a [Texas Tech University interview](https://www.depts.ttu.edu/comc/about/news/posts/2022/09/Faculty-Research-Sees-Worldwide-Circulation.php): "we don't want to tell people to turn off the news." The team pointed towards mindful news use rather than quitting.

Many people do switch off for a while. The Reuters Institute's [Digital News Report 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary) found that 40% of people across markets sometimes or often avoid the news, up from 29% in 2017. A short break is reasonable. But the news you skip still shapes your rent, your job market, your investments and the conversations around you. A healthy diet aims for a steady, smaller intake, not a fast.

## What goes on a balanced news plate?

Here is the food comparison made practical.

| On the plate | The news version | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staples | Two or three trusted sources you return to | You stop seeing the same scare ten times |
| Vegetables | Explanations and analysis of a few big stories | You understand what happened and why |
| Protein | News about your own field or city | It is the news you can actually act on |
| Something different | One source outside your usual view | It keeps you out of a bubble |
| Snacks | Headlines, alerts, social posts | Fine in small amounts, not as a meal |
| Junk | Outrage threads, rumours, forwards with no source | High in emotion, low in facts |

A few rules sit underneath the table:

- **Set mealtimes.** Check at one or two fixed times a day. Our post on [staying informed in 5 minutes a day](/blog/stay-informed-in-5-minutes-a-day) has a simple routine.
- **Have an end.** A newsletter, a bulletin or a short set of videos finishes. A feed does not.
- **Separate fact from opinion.** Commentary has its place, but it should not be most of the plate.
- **Check before you share.** News Co/Lab suggests four quick questions: who made it, what is the evidence, why was it made and what context is missing.

## How does this look for a news reader in India?

The Reuters Institute's [India page](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/india) for 2025 gives a picture of where people get their news. YouTube (55%), WhatsApp (46%), Instagram (37%) and Facebook (36%) were the most used platforms for news. WhatsApp was also the channel most often seen as a source of false information, named by 53% of respondents. Overall trust in news stood at 43%.

One caveat: that survey covers mainly English-speaking, online news users, and the report says it is not nationally representative. Still, for the young city readers it does describe, the pattern is clear. Much of the news arrives through platforms built for entertainment and private chats. That is exactly where snacks and junk crowd out the real meal.

A few small changes help:

1. Treat a forward as a headline, not a fact. If it matters, find the story at a named outlet.
2. Pick one source for business and one for the world. Our guides on [Indian business news](/blog/best-ways-to-follow-indian-business-news) and [following geopolitics](/blog/how-to-follow-geopolitics) list options, including ones we do not make.
3. Keep your own city on the plate. Local stories about traffic, water, housing and jobs often touch your life more than a global headline.

## A sample news diet for a busy week

This is a starting point, not a prescription. Adjust the times to your day.

| When | What | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekday mornings | One summary of the day's main stories | 5 minutes |
| Weekday commute or lunch | One deeper read or video on a story you care about | 10 minutes |
| Weekday evenings | News on your field, if anything changed | 5 minutes |
| One weekend slot | A longer weekly review, plus one piece from outside your usual view | 30 minutes |
| Last hour before sleep | No news | the whole hour |

That adds up to well under three hours a week. It keeps you informed enough to talk about what matters and make decisions, without living inside the feed. If the late-night scroll is your weak spot, read our guide to [doomscrolling at night](/blog/doomscrolling-at-night).

## How do you know your news diet is off?

Ask yourself a few honest questions:

- Do you check the news without deciding to, many times a day?
- Do you finish a session feeling worse but no better informed?
- Can you explain the main story of the week in two sentences? If not, you may be grazing headlines instead of reading.
- Is news eating into sleep, work or time with people?
- Do most of your stories reach you through forwards and comments rather than sources you chose?

One or two yeses is normal. Most of them suggests it is time to cut the snacks and set mealtimes. Our post on [following the news without the anxiety](/blog/follow-the-news-without-the-anxiety) has more on the mood side.

## When should you talk to someone?

Feeling upset by upsetting events is normal. If worry about the news is affecting your sleep, work or relationships for weeks, or you cannot stop checking even when you want to, speak to a doctor or a counsellor. That is a sensible step, not an overreaction.
