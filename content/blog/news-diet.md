**A news diet is the mix of news you take in: which sources you use, how often you check them, in what format and for how long.** Like a food diet, it is not about cutting news out. A healthy news diet is small, varied and regular: a few trusted sources, set times to check, and more explanation than alarm.

That is the whole idea in three sentences. The rest of this post gives you a simple 7-day plan to build one, what a balanced week looks like after that, and what the research says about too much news (and too little).

## What does a news diet mean?

The phrase borrows from food on purpose. Nobody asks whether food is good or bad for you. They ask what kind, how much and when. A news diet asks the same questions about current events:

- **Sources:** which outlets, apps, channels and people you get news from
- **Frequency:** how many times a day you check
- **Format:** text, video, audio, alerts, forwards
- **Amount:** how much time it all takes
- **Balance:** the mix of local, national, world and your own field

Library and media groups use the same idea. A [guide from the University of the District of Columbia](https://udc.libguides.com/blog/Creating-Your-News-Diet) suggests one international, one national, one local and one hyperlocal source. [News Co/Lab](https://newscollab.org/2018/12/24/you-are-what-you-read-why-a-balanced-information-diet-matters/) warns that leaning on one outlet or platform makes for a poor diet.

Everyone already has a news diet. The only question is whether you chose it, or the feed chose it for you.

## How to start a news diet: a 7-day plan

You do not need an app or a rulebook. One small step a day is enough. Each step builds on the one before.

| Day | What to do | Time it takes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day 1 | Notice where your news comes from today. Jot down each app, channel, group or person. | 5 minutes |
| Day 2 | Pick two or three trusted sources to be your staples. At least one should be a named outlet, not a social feed. | 10 minutes |
| Day 3 | Set mealtimes: one or two fixed times to check the news. Turn off most news alerts on your phone. | 5 minutes |
| Day 4 | Add one source from outside your usual view, and one about your city or your field. | 10 minutes |
| Day 5 | Swap one headline scroll for one deeper read or video on a story you care about. | 10 minutes |
| Day 6 | Check before you share. Treat every forward as a headline, not a fact, until you find it at a named source. | As needed |
| Day 7 | Look back. Can you explain the main story of the week in two sentences? Keep what helped, drop what did not. | 10 minutes |

Two rules make the plan stick:

1. **Choose sources that end.** A newsletter, a bulletin or a short set of videos finishes. A feed does not.
2. **Keep the last hour before sleep news-free.** Late-night checking is where most people slip. Our guide to [doomscrolling at night](/blog/doomscrolling-at-night) has more on this.

## What goes on a balanced news plate?

Here is the food comparison made practical.

| On the plate | The news version | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staples | Two or three trusted sources you return to | You stop seeing the same scare ten times |
| Vegetables | Explanations and analysis of a few big stories | You understand what happened and why |
| Protein | News about your own field or city | It is the news you can act on |
| Something different | One source outside your usual view | It keeps you out of a bubble |
| Snacks | Headlines, alerts, social posts | Fine in small amounts, not as a meal |
| Junk | Outrage threads, rumours, forwards with no source | High in emotion, low in facts |

Commentary has its place, but it should not be most of the plate. When you do want to check a story, News Co/Lab suggests four quick questions: who made it, what is the evidence, why was it made and what context is missing.

## What does a healthy news week look like after that?

Once the 7 days are done, a normal week can look like this. It is a starting point, not a prescription. Adjust the times to your day.

| When | What | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekday mornings | One summary of the day's main stories | 5 minutes |
| Weekday commute or lunch | One deeper read or video on a story you care about | 10 minutes |
| Weekday evenings | News on your field, if anything changed | 5 minutes |
| One weekend slot | A longer weekly review, plus one piece from outside your usual view | 30 minutes |
| Last hour before sleep | No news | the whole hour |

That adds up to well under three hours a week. It keeps you informed enough to talk about what matters and make decisions, without living inside the feed. For a shorter version, see our routine for [staying informed in 5 minutes a day](/blog/stay-informed-in-5-minutes-a-day).

## Is a news diet healthy? What the research says

The research points one way, with an important caveat: most of it is correlational. It shows that heavy news use and stress go together, not that one causes the other.

**After the Boston Marathon bombings.** In a [2013 study in PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3890785), Holman, Garfin and Silver surveyed 4,675 American adults two to four weeks after the attack. People who reported six or more hours a day of bombing-related media in the week after were far more likely to report high acute stress than those with little exposure. The authors say plainly that their study cannot prove cause, and that the link may run both ways.

**Problematic news habits.** In a [2022 study in Health Communication](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/08/220824102936.htm), McLaughlin, Gotlieb and Mills surveyed 1,100 US adults. They classed 16.5% as having severely problematic news consumption: feeling absorbed by the news, struggling to stop and finding it got in the way of daily life. Of that group, 73.6% reported mental ill-being "quite a bit" or "very much", compared with 8% of everyone else. Again, this shows an association, not proof.

**Too little news has costs too.** One of the 2022 researchers, Melissa Gotlieb, said in a [Texas Tech University interview](https://www.depts.ttu.edu/comc/about/news/posts/2022/09/Faculty-Research-Sees-Worldwide-Circulation.php): "we don't want to tell people to turn off the news." The Reuters Institute's [Digital News Report 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary) found that 40% of people across markets sometimes or often avoid the news, up from 29% in 2017. A short break is reasonable. But the news you skip still shapes your rent, your job market, your money and the conversations around you.

So a planned news diet is the healthy middle: smaller and steadier than a constant feed, but not a fast. For a wider look at the evidence on scrolling, see [is doomscrolling bad for you?](/blog/is-doomscrolling-bad-for-you)

## A news diet for readers in India

The Reuters Institute's [India page](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/india) for 2025 shows where people get their news. YouTube (55%), WhatsApp (46%), Instagram (37%) and Facebook (36%) were the most used platforms for news. WhatsApp was also the channel most often seen as a source of false information, named by 53% of respondents. Overall trust in news stood at 43%.

That survey covers mainly English-speaking, online news users, and the report says it is not nationally representative. Still, the pattern is clear: much of the news arrives through platforms built for entertainment and private chats. That is where snacks and junk crowd out the meal. Three small changes help:

1. Treat a forward as a headline, not a fact. If it matters, find the story at a named outlet.
2. Pick one source for business and one for the world. Our guides on [Indian business news](/blog/best-ways-to-follow-indian-business-news) and [following geopolitics](/blog/how-to-follow-geopolitics) list options, including ones we do not make.
3. Keep your own city on the plate. Local stories about traffic, water, housing and jobs often touch your life more than a global headline.

## Signs your news diet is off

Ask yourself a few honest questions:

- Do you check the news without deciding to, many times a day?
- Do you finish a session feeling worse but no better informed?
- Can you explain the main story of the week in two sentences?
- Is news eating into sleep, work or time with people?
- Do most of your stories reach you through forwards and comments rather than sources you chose?

One or two warning signs is normal. Most of them suggests it is time to go back to Day 1 of the plan. Our post on [following the news without the anxiety](/blog/follow-the-news-without-the-anxiety) has more on the mood side.

If worry about the news is affecting your sleep, work or relationships for weeks, or you cannot stop checking even when you want to, speak to a doctor or a counsellor. That is a sensible step, not an overreaction.
