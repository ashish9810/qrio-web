**The short answer:** the best way to follow Indian business news is to build a small stack instead of picking one winner. Use one short daily briefing, one deeper publication for the why, and the official sources for anything that moves money.

That takes about ten minutes a day. It also keeps you out of the feed, which is where most of the noise lives. Here is how to build it, with the honest trade-offs of each choice.

## Why is Indian business news so noisy?

There is simply a lot of it, and much of it competes for your attention rather than your understanding. Alerts, forwarded messages and video clips all push the same story at you ten times.

The Reuters Institute's [Digital News Report 2025 India page](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/india) gives a sense of where people meet news. Among its Indian respondents, 55% said they access news through YouTube, 46% through WhatsApp, 37% through Instagram and 36% through Facebook. The same page says 53% named WhatsApp as the biggest misinformation threat, the highest of any market in the survey.

One caution. The report says its India sample is mainly English-speaking online users, so it is not nationally representative. It likely under-represents TV and print. Read the numbers as a picture of people like you, not of the whole country.

If the volume itself is wearing you down, you are in good company. Across all markets, the [report's executive summary](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary) says 40% of people sometimes or often avoid the news. Our post on [following the news without the anxiety](/blog/follow-the-news-without-the-anxiety) covers that side.

## What should a good business news stack include?

Think in three layers. Each does a different job, and you do not need more than one source per layer.

| Layer | Job | Examples | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daily briefing | Know what happened today in a few minutes | Finshots | Free subscription |
| Deeper reading | Understand why it happened and what comes next | The Morning Context, The Ken | Free tier and paid |
| Original sources | See the real announcement, not a summary of it | RBI, SEBI | Free |

Everything below is based on what each publisher says about itself on its own site. We have not tested them against each other, so treat this as a map, not a ranking.

## Which daily briefing is worth reading?

A briefing should end. That is the whole point.

[Finshots](https://finshots.in/) describes itself as "Financial News Made Simple" and says it delivers the most important financial developments in plain English, in under three minutes. Its homepage reports more than 5,00,000 subscribers. It runs a daily edition plus sections on markets and personal money. The homepage does not list a price, and it offers a subscribe button, so free access looks likely, but check when you sign up.

The big business dailies and sites, such as Mint, the Economic Times, Business Standard and Moneycontrol, are also worth knowing. We could not open several of their pages while researching this post, so we are not going to describe their paywalls or features. Check each one directly and see which style suits you. The test is simple: can you finish today's edition and feel done?

## Where do I go when I want the why?

For the longer view, two Indian publications describe their approach clearly.

[The Morning Context](https://themorningcontext.com/) says it offers detailed stories on technology startups, business and economic current affairs. Its site lists coverage of startups and internet businesses, banking and mutual funds, and a Gulf desk with its own newsletter. It offers a free subscription as well as paid plans, including student options. The site does not say which stories sit behind the paywall.

[The Ken](https://the-ken.com/) says it has published analytical journalism on business and startups across India since 2016. It covers areas such as fintech, SaaS, retail, venture capital and public policy, and also runs newsletters and podcasts. Most of its content needs a paid subscription. It offers a 30-day free trial, lets you unlock some stories by signing up with an email, and says some of its best work is free.

Paying is a real choice. The Reuters Institute's [executive summary](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary) says 18% of people in a basket of 20 richer countries paid for online news in the last year. India is not in that basket, so we cannot give you a figure for India. A simple rule: pay for one publication only if you finish its pieces and use what you read.

## Why bother with the official sources?

Because the headline is often a summary of someone else's announcement. When the news is about interest rates, a new rule or a market order, the original is short and free.

- The [RBI press releases page](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx) lists releases in reverse date order, with archives going back to 1990. Recent entries cover things like liquidity auctions, government securities auctions and foreign exchange measures.
- SEBI's [media and notifications section](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebiweb/home/HomeAction.do?doListing=yes&sid=6&ssid=0&smid=0) lists press releases, speeches and public notices. You can filter by date and keyword and sign up for email updates.

You will not read these daily. Open them when a story affects you, such as a rate decision or a rule about your investments, and read the first paragraph. It is often clearer than the coverage around it.

## How do I put it together in ten minutes?

A routine you can keep beats a perfect one you drop.

1. **Morning, three to five minutes:** open your daily briefing. Read it to the end. Close it.
2. **Once or twice a week, ten minutes:** read one deeper piece on a topic you care about, such as a company in your sector.
3. **When something big breaks:** open the RBI or SEBI page and read the original once.
4. **Turn off the rest:** keep one or two alerts at most. Mute business forwards on WhatsApp.

For a fuller routine, see [how to stay informed in 5 minutes a day](/blog/stay-informed-in-5-minutes-a-day). If scrolling is the habit you want to swap out, [the alternative to doomscrolling](/blog/alternative-to-doomscrolling) has seven ideas.

## Where do short videos fit?

They can be a good first pass. A short video is quick to watch and easy to remember, which helps when you are new to a topic like a funding round or a policy change.

They are not a substitute for the original. Videos compress, and compression can drop context. Use a video to know that something happened, then use your deeper read or the official page for anything you plan to act on.

We make Qrio, a free Android app of short videos on business, startups and the world, so we are biased here. Treat it as one possible first pass, not the whole stack. Our post on [whether short videos are bad for you](/blog/are-short-videos-bad-for-you) looks at the research fairly.

## A note on what this list leaves out

There are many good sources not named here, including regional-language outlets, TV channels, podcasts and the large business dailies we could not fully check. Our aim is to show the shape of a good stack, not to name every ingredient. If a source helps you finish the day informed and calm, it belongs in yours.
