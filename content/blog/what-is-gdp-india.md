**The short answer:** GDP, or gross domestic product, is the total value of everything an economy produces in a period. When the news says "India grew 7.8%", it means real GDP, adjusted for price rises, was 7.8% higher than in the same quarter a year earlier.

That is the figure India reported for April to June 2026. The next one, for July to September, is due from the Ministry of Statistics (MoSPI) on 30 November 2026, so this is a good week to know how to read it. This guide covers what GDP measures, real vs nominal growth, what changed with the new 2022-23 series, and a five-step way to read the release. It is general information, not financial advice.

## What is GDP, in plain words?

Imagine adding up the value of every haircut, phone, tonne of wheat, software contract and train ticket produced in India in three months. Then remove the value of the inputs used to make them, so nothing is counted twice. What is left is GDP.

There are three ways to measure it, and in theory they all give the same answer:

- **Production side.** Add the value added by each sector: farms, factories, construction, services. This tells you *which industries* drove growth.
- **Expenditure side.** Add what everyone spent: households, government, businesses investing, plus exports minus imports. This tells you *what kind of demand* drove growth.
- **Income side.** Add wages, profits and rent earned.

The [World Bank's April 2026 India Development Update](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/4262e1e15b463ecb360cec4ad78cf062-0310012026/original/April-2026-India-Development-Update.pdf) explains that the production and expenditure totals never match perfectly in practice. The gap shows up in the data as "discrepancies". Small gaps are normal. Big ones are a sign to treat the demand-side numbers with care.

## What is the difference between real and nominal GDP?

**Nominal GDP** values output at current prices. If India made exactly the same things this year but prices rose 5%, nominal GDP would rise 5% even though nothing more was produced.

**Real GDP** values output at base-year prices, so it only rises when the economy actually produces more. That is why the headline growth number is always real growth.

The gap between the two is roughly the inflation in the whole economy, called the GDP deflator. In April to June 2026, nominal GDP grew 10.3% and real GDP grew 7.8%, according to [PolicyEdge](https://www.policyedge.in/p/indias-gdp-growth-accelerates-to-78-in-q1-fy27-as-investment-and-services-strengthen) and [India Macro Indicators](https://indiamacroindicators.co.in/resources/blogs/india-q1-fy27-gdp-growth), both citing MoSPI. That implies economy-wide prices rose a little over 2%, well below retail inflation. Retail inflation tracks what households pay, which is a different basket. Our guide to [CPI inflation](/blog/what-is-cpi-inflation-india) covers that number.

Nominal GDP still matters. Tax collections, company revenues and ratios like the fiscal deficit are all measured against it. PolicyEdge puts nominal GDP for the quarter at ₹88.27 lakh crore.

## What is the difference between GDP and GVA?

You will see two growth numbers in every release. **GVA** (gross value added) is the production-side total: what each sector adds. **GDP** is GVA plus taxes on products, minus subsidies on products.

Most economists watch GVA to judge how the underlying economy is doing, because tax and subsidy swings can move GDP for reasons unrelated to output. In April to June 2026, real GVA grew 8.2% while real GDP grew 7.8%. PolicyEdge attributes the gap to net taxes growing only 3.9% in real terms.

## What changed with the new 2022-23 GDP series?

In February 2026, MoSPI replaced the old GDP series, which used 2011-12 as its base year, with a new one based on 2022-23. The World Bank's update describes the main changes:

- **Newer data sources**, such as tax records, business registries and company filings.
- **Better price measures**, including new producer price indices and a revamped CPI, used to turn nominal values into real ones.
- **Updated supply and use tables**, to narrow the gap between the production and expenditure sides.
- **A smaller measured economy.** Unusually for a rebasing, nominal GDP levels were revised *down* by 3 to 4% in each fiscal year from FY23, mainly because the size of the informal economy was reassessed.

Growth rates moved too. The World Bank's table on the new series shows real growth of 7.2% in FY24, 7.1% in FY25 and 7.6% in FY26. It says the revised numbers show growth was "less volatile and more broad-based than previously believed."

One method change explains a confusing headline from September. Manufacturing now uses **double deflation**, which adjusts outputs and inputs with separate price indices. [PSU Watch reported](https://psuwatch.com/newsupdates/govt-explains-gdp-math-why-manufacturing-shows-negative-inflation-and-why-last-years-gdp-was-revised) MoSPI's explanation of why the manufacturing deflator came out at minus 1.5% for April to June 2026: input costs rose faster than output prices. That pushed real manufacturing growth (9.2%) above nominal growth (7.7%). MoSPI said this does not mean cars, steel or clothes got cheaper.

**The practical rule:** never compare a number from the new series with one from the old. PSU Watch notes that nominal GDP for April to June 2025 was ₹86.05 lakh crore in the old series but ₹80.32 lakh crore in the new one. Some commentators compared the new Q1 FY27 figure with the old Q1 FY26 figure and got a much lower growth rate. MoSPI rejected that comparison, because it mixes two different rulers.

## How do you read the quarterly GDP release in five minutes?

MoSPI publishes quarterly GDP about two months after each quarter ends. India's fiscal year runs from April to March, so Q2 FY27 means July to September 2026. Here is what to look at, in order:

1. **The headline.** Real GDP growth for the quarter, year on year. For Q1 FY27: 7.8%, up from 6.9% a year earlier.
2. **Real GVA.** Is the underlying economy growing faster or slower than the headline? Q1 FY27: 8.2%.
3. **The sector table.** Which parts of the economy pulled growth up or down.
4. **The expenditure table.** Is growth coming from households, investment or the government?
5. **Revisions.** Earlier quarters get revised. A strong new number with a weaker revised previous quarter tells a different story.

Here is how steps 3 and 4 looked for April to June 2026. Figures are real growth, year on year, from PolicyEdge and India Macro Indicators, which match each other:

| Part of the economy | Q1 FY27 growth (%) | Q1 FY26 growth (%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agriculture, forestry and fishing | 3.6 | 4.4 |
| Mining and quarrying | -2.4 | 12.4 |
| Manufacturing | 9.2 | 8.3 |
| Construction | 7.7 | 5.2 |
| Financial, real estate, IT and professional services | 12.1 | 8.8 |
| Private consumption (PFCE) | 7.1 | 6.8 |
| Government consumption (GFCE) | 4.3 | 4.5 |
| Investment (GFCF) | 11.9 | 5.8 |
| Exports | 12.0 | 6.0 |

Read the table like this. Services and investment did the heavy lifting. Investment rose to about 34% of nominal GDP, according to PolicyEdge. Farming and mining were weak. Private consumption, which PolicyEdge puts at 55.6% of nominal GDP, grew steadily but not spectacularly. One quarter is a data point. Watch whether the same pattern holds next quarter.

## What to watch on 30 November 2026

Forecasts for July to September 2026 sit below the last quarter's 7.8%:

- **RBI:** 7.2%, in its [policy statement of 7 October 2026](https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=63742). It projects 7.1% for the full year.
- **ICRA:** 7.5%, which would be a five-quarter low, with GVA at 7.2% and farm growth near zero because of a weak monsoon, [KNN India reported](https://knnindia.co.in/news/newsdetails/economy/indias-q2-fy27-gdp-growth-may-ease-to-75-on-weak-farm-output-hit-five-quarter-low-icra) on 18 November.
- **SBI Research:** about 7.8%, with GVA at 8 to 8.2%, [according to ANI via Devdiscourse](https://www.devdiscourse.com/article/business/4005076-indias-q2-fy27-gdp-growth-pegged-at-78-gva-growth-seen-at-8-82-sbi-research).
- **Finance Ministry:** about 7.3%, from its nowcasting model, [Outlook reported](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/indias-q2-gdp-growth-seen-at-73-by-finance-ministry-ahead-of-rbi-estimate).

These are forecasts, not data. When the number comes out, check three things: whether agriculture shrank as feared, whether investment kept up its pace, and whether nominal growth rose as prices climbed. The RBI's next rate decision follows on 2 to 4 December, so the GDP figure will shape that debate alongside inflation. ## What does a GDP number mean for you?

GDP is an average for the whole country, so it will never match your own year. But it tells you useful things:

- **Jobs and pay.** Fast growth in services and manufacturing usually means more hiring in those fields.
- **Company earnings.** Nominal GDP growth is a rough ceiling for how fast the average company's revenue can grow over time. Our guide on [reading quarterly results](/blog/how-to-read-quarterly-results) shows how to check one company against that backdrop.
- **Markets are not the economy.** A strong GDP print does not guarantee a stock rally. We covered exactly that in our story on why [India grew at 7.8% and its stock market shrugged](/explainers/india-grew-at-78-and-its-stock-market-shrugged).

If you only have five minutes on release day, read the headline, the GVA number and the sector table. For the rest of the week's news, our guide to [staying informed in 5 minutes a day](/blog/stay-informed-in-5-minutes-a-day) has a routine.

## What is still uncertain

Quarterly GDP is an early estimate and gets revised, sometimes by a lot. The new series is less than a year old, so analysts are still learning how it behaves, especially the double-deflation effect on manufacturing. MoSPI said it would publish a detailed sources and methods document, PolicyEdge reported. Until then, treat sharp swings in single sectors with some caution.
