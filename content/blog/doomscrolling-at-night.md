**The short answer:** to stop doomscrolling at night, change the setup, not your willpower. Charge the phone outside the bedroom, stop scrolling 30 to 60 minutes before bed, and give that last hour a different job, because by bedtime your self-control is at its weakest and the feed is designed never to end.

Night mode will not save you. The research suggests the problem is less the light from the screen and more what the screen keeps you doing. Here is why the bedtime scroll is so sticky, and what actually helps.

## Why is it so hard to stop scrolling at night?

There is a name for it. In 2014, Floor Kroese and colleagues at Utrecht University introduced the idea of [bedtime procrastination](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00611/full): failing to go to bed at the time you intended, when nothing outside you is stopping you.

They surveyed 177 adults online. Three findings stand out:

- People with **lower self-regulation** put off bedtime more.
- Bedtime procrastination predicted **less sleep, more daytime tiredness and more nights of not enough sleep**, even after accounting for age, gender and self-regulation.
- Almost 30% of the sample said they slept **six hours or less** on weeknights.

The authors are clear that this was a one-time survey, so it shows a link, not cause and effect. Short sleep could also wear down self-control, rather than the other way round. But the pattern will feel familiar. At 11:30 pm you are tired, your guard is down, and a feed that never ends is one tap away.

We go deeper into the psychology in our post on [why we doomscroll](/blog/why-we-doomscroll).

## What is revenge bedtime procrastination?

It is the same habit with a motive. The phrase comes from a Chinese expression usually translated as "retaliatory staying up late". The [Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-hygiene/revenge-bedtime-procrastination) describes it as staying up on purpose to get personal time, even when you know it will hurt your sleep. It often shows up in people who feel they have little control over their days.

If your workday runs from the first call to the last Slack message, the hour after everyone else is asleep can feel like the only hour that belongs to you. Scrolling is the easiest way to spend it. That matters for the fix: if you just ban the phone and leave that need unmet, the habit tends to come back.

## How common is losing sleep to your phone?

Very. In a 2022 survey of 2,010 US adults commissioned by the [American Academy of Sleep Medicine](https://aasm.org/are-you-tiktok-tired-93-of-gen-z-admit-to-staying-up-past-their-bedtime-due-to-social-media/) (AASM), 93% of Gen Z said they had lost sleep by staying up past their bedtime on social media. Across all ages the figure was 80%.

An older Belgian study of 844 adults, published in [*Social Science & Medicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26688552/) in 2016, found that six in ten took their phone to the bedroom. Using it after lights out was linked to worse sleep quality, more insomnia symptoms and more fatigue. Again, that is a correlation from a survey. People who already sleep badly may simply reach for the phone more.

## Is it the blue light, or something else?

Most advice on this topic starts with blue light. The evidence for the classic fix, night mode, is weaker than you might expect.

In a study published in *Sleep Health* in 2021, Chad Jensen at Brigham Young University and colleagues at Cincinnati Children's Hospital split 167 people aged 18 to 24 into three groups. One group used their iPhone before bed with Night Shift on. One used it with Night Shift off. One used no phone before bed. Everyone wore a wrist tracker to measure their sleep.

[According to BYU](https://news.byu.edu/intellect/is-night-shift-really-helping-you-sleep-better), there were no differences across the three groups overall. Among people who slept around seven hours a night, those who skipped the phone slept slightly better than both phone groups. Night Shift made no difference either way. Jensen's point was that we should ask how much of the stimulation comes from the light and how much from the mental engagement of what we are doing.

That fits common experience. A tense news thread or an argument in the comments wakes your brain up whatever colour the screen is. So the useful question is not "how do I make my screen warmer?" but "how do I stop starting the scroll?"

## Does putting the phone down before bed actually help?

There is some early evidence that it does. In a [2020 randomised pilot trial in *PLOS ONE*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7010281/), Jing-wen He and colleagues in Shanghai worked with 38 college students who slept poorly and used their phones in bed. Half were asked to stop using the phone 30 minutes before their usual bedtime. The other half got no instructions.

The group that put the phone down reported:

- Falling asleep faster, from about 31 minutes to about 18 minutes on average
- Sleeping a little longer, from about 6.2 to 6.6 hours
- Better sleep quality scores and feeling less wound up before sleep

Treat this as promising, not proven. The sample was tiny, everyone was motivated to cut down, and sleep was measured by diaries and questionnaires rather than trackers. The authors note that an earlier trial with teenage athletes found no benefit from limiting screens after 10 pm. Still, it matches the official advice: the AASM suggests turning off electronics [at least 30 minutes to an hour before bed](https://aasm.org/are-you-tiktok-tired-93-of-gen-z-admit-to-staying-up-past-their-bedtime-due-to-social-media/), and the Sleep Foundation gives similar guidance.

## How do you break the bedtime scroll?

Since tired brains are bad at saying no, the trick is to make the scroll harder to start and the alternative easier. Pick two or three of these, not all of them.

| What keeps you scrolling | What to try instead |
| --- | --- |
| The phone is in your hand in bed | Charge it outside the bedroom, or at least out of arm's reach. Use a cheap alarm clock. |
| "Just one more" never ends | Set a fixed phone curfew 30 to 60 minutes before bed, and use your phone's own bedtime or downtime setting to grey out apps. |
| Notifications pull you back in | Turn on Do Not Disturb at the same time every night, with exceptions for family. |
| You need time that is yours | Move it earlier. Protect 30 minutes in the evening for something you enjoy, so the night is not your only window. |
| Your mind races with tomorrow's tasks | Write tomorrow's to-do list on paper before bed, so you are not rehearsing it in the dark. |
| You want to catch up on the news | Do it earlier in the evening, in one bounded session, with something that has an end. |

If you do want to check the day's news, do it well before bed and choose a format that finishes. Our post on [how to stop doomscrolling](/blog/how-to-stop-doomscrolling) covers the daytime side of the habit, and our list of [alternatives to doomscrolling](/blog/alternative-to-doomscrolling) has ideas for what to do with your hands instead.

## What if you are lying awake and want to reach for the phone?

This is where many people slip. You turn the light off, sleep does not come, and the phone feels like the only thing to do.

Sleep clinicians use a technique called stimulus control, which is part of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia. The idea is to keep your bed linked with sleep, not with being awake. A [stimulus control handout from Penn Medicine's sleep programme](https://www.med.upenn.edu/cbti/assets/user-content/documents/Stimulus%20Control%20Handout.doc) suggests:

1. If you have been awake in bed for roughly 15 to 20 minutes, get up and go to another room.
2. Do something quiet and calm for a while, away from phones, tablets and bright light.
3. Go back to bed when you feel sleepy, and repeat if needed.
4. Use the bed only for sleep and sex, not for scrolling, emailing or worrying.

[CommonSpirit Health](https://www.commonspirit.org/conditions-treatments/stimulus-control-therapy) describes the same core rules: go to bed when sleepy, keep the bedroom for sleep, and get up to do something relaxing when you cannot sleep. A paperback and a dim lamp in the living room beat a glowing feed under the blanket.

## When is it more than a bad habit?

Sometimes the late-night scroll is a symptom, not the cause. If you lie awake most nights for weeks, wake up exhausted however long you sleep, or scroll because worry will not let you switch off, talk to a doctor or a counsellor. Insomnia, anxiety and low mood are all treatable, and a professional will help more than any app setting.

For most of us, though, the fix is simpler and a little boring. Put the phone somewhere else, give the last hour of the day a different job, and let the feed keep going without you.
