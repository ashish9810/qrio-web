**The short answer:** the best apps to replace your Instagram scroll are ones that give you something you can remember afterwards, such as a news summary, a Wikipedia article, a TED talk, a book summary or a short skill lesson. Pick one that fits the five idle minutes you already have, and put it where Instagram used to sit on your home screen.

You do not have to quit Instagram to do this. The goal is smaller. When you reach for your phone on autopilot, the thing you open should leave you a little better informed, not just a little more tired.

## Why replace the Instagram scroll at all?

Because the short-video feed is built to keep going. There is no last video. You stop when something outside the app stops you.

The research on short videos is not settled. Our post on [whether short videos are bad for you](/blog/are-short-videos-bad-for-you) walks through it. The short version is that heavy use is linked to weaker attention in many studies, but most of those studies cannot prove the videos cause it.

So this is not a health argument. It is a simpler one. If you are going to spend ten minutes on your phone anyway, you may as well walk away with one useful thing.

## What makes a good replacement app?

A good swap matches the habit, not just the topic. Look for four things:

- **It is short.** You reach for Instagram in small gaps. The replacement must fit a two to fifteen minute gap, or you will not use it.
- **It ends.** A clear finish, like the end of a summary or a lesson, makes it easier to put the phone down.
- **It leaves something behind.** A fact, an idea or a skill you can use or mention later.
- **It is pleasant to open.** If it feels like homework, you will slide back to the old app within a week.

Nothing on the list below is perfect on all four. That is fine. Choose by what you want to come away with.

### Inshorts, for news in under a minute

[Inshorts](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.nis.app) is a short-news app. Its Play Store listing says it picks news from national and international sources and summarises each story in 60 words or less, in English or Hindi. The listing says stories carry "only headlines and facts, no opinions."

It is the closest swap for the swipe habit itself. You flick through cards much as you would through videos. The difference is that each card tells you something that happened. The risk is the same as any feed: it can keep going. Decide on a number of cards before you start.

### Wikipedia, for following your curiosity

The [official Wikipedia app](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=org.wikipedia) is made by the Wikimedia Foundation, a nonprofit. Its listing says it is free and ad-free, and lets you search more than 40 million articles in over 300 languages. You can save articles to reading lists and read them offline. The [Wikimedia apps page](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Wikimedia_Apps) also notes the apps are ad-free and support more than 300 languages.

This one works best if you keep a running list of things you wondered about during the day. When the urge to scroll hits, read one of them instead. One article has a clear end, and it is often more surprising than the video you would have watched.

### TED, for one big idea

The [TED app](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.ted.android) says it has more than 3,000 talks, with subtitles in over 100 languages, and lets you download talks to watch or hear offline. It also includes podcasts from the TED Audio Collective.

TED is still video, which makes it an easy step from a short-video habit. But one talk has a beginning, a middle and an end. Watching one complete argument is a very different experience from thirty clips with no thread between them.

### Blinkist and Headway, for books in 15 minutes

[Blinkist](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.blinkslabs.blinkist.android) says it has summaries of more than 7,500 books and podcasts, each taking about 15 minutes, which you can read or listen to. [Headway](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.headway.books) offers summaries of more than 2,000 non-fiction titles, also in about 15 minutes, and pitches itself directly at people tired of "mindless doom-scrolling."

Both are useful for getting the main idea of a book you were never going to finish. Be fair to the books, though. A summary is a map, not the trip. If one grabs you, read the real thing. Both apps are built around paid subscriptions, so check the price and trial terms inside the app before you sign up.

### Duolingo and Brilliant, for a skill

[Duolingo](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.duolingo) says it teaches more than 40 languages through short lessons, and now adds maths, music and chess. Its Play Store page shows over 500 million downloads. [Brilliant](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=org.brilliant.android) focuses on maths and coding, with hands-on problems in place of lectures.

These are the most active options here. You answer, you get it wrong, you try again. That makes them harder to use on autopilot, which is partly the point. A five-minute streak a day adds up across months.

### Qrio, for short videos about business and the world

If you like the video format and just want the content to be worth more, this is the gap we are trying to fill. Each video explains one story, such as a company, a deal or a policy, in a short video, and the day's set has an end. We are not neutral here, and nothing in this post tests our approach. Use whichever app on this list suits you.

## Can you just limit Instagram instead?

Yes, and a limit plus a swap usually works better than either alone. A limit stops the scroll. A swap gives your thumb somewhere else to go.

Three built-in tools are worth knowing:

1. **Android app timers.** Google's [Digital Wellbeing help page](https://support.google.com/android/answer/9346420?hl=en) explains how to see how long you use each app and set app timers. You can even add a PIN so the limit is harder to undo in a weak moment. Steps vary by phone.
2. **YouTube's Shorts timer.** In October 2025 YouTube began rolling out a daily limit for the Shorts feed in its mobile app, as reported by [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/22/youtube-adds-at-timer-for-you-to-stop-scrolling-shorts) and [9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2025/10/22/youtubes-new-daily-timer-stops-you-from-wasting-all-your-time-watching-shorts/). When you hit the limit, a prompt pauses the feed. Both reports note you can dismiss it, so it is a nudge, not a lock.
3. **Instagram's Quiet mode.** Announced in January 2023, Quiet mode silences notifications, sends an auto-reply to messages and shows friends you are away, according to [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/01/19/instagrams-new-quiet-mode-helps-you-take-a-break-from-the-app) and [Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/media/instagram-how-to-turn-on-quiet-mode/). Adweek says the default schedule is 11 pm to 7 am. It cuts the pull to open the app, though it does not stop you scrolling once you are inside.

Menus move around between app versions, so if a setting is not where a guide says, search for it inside the app's settings.

## How do you make the switch stick?

Most people do not fail because they picked the wrong app. They fail because the old habit is one tap away. A few small changes help:

- **Swap the home screen spot.** Move Instagram into a folder on the second screen and put your new app where it used to be. Your thumb goes to the same place out of habit.
- **Pick one app, not five.** Installing everything above is a nice afternoon project that rarely survives the week. Start with one and add a second only if the first sticks.
- **Match the app to the moment.** News cards for the queue at the coffee counter, a book summary for the commute, a lesson before bed.
- **Give it an end.** Ten cards, one article, one lesson. Then close it.

If the pull to scroll feels stronger than a habit, our guide on [how to stop doomscrolling](/blog/how-to-stop-doomscrolling) goes further. And if you want ideas that involve no screen at all, see our list of [things to do when you reach for your phone](/blog/alternative-to-doomscrolling). If scrolling is affecting your sleep, mood or work in a way you cannot shift, it is worth talking to a doctor or counsellor.
