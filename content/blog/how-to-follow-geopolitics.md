**The short answer:** you do not need to read the news all day to follow geopolitics. Pick two or three regions that touch your life, ask four simple questions of every big story, and spend one longer slot a week on background instead of chasing live updates.

That is enough to understand why things happen, which is the part most people feel they are missing. Here is how to set it up, where to look, and what to ignore.

## Why does geopolitics feel so hard to follow?

Because most coverage is written for people who already know the backstory. A headline about a strait, a sanction or a summit assumes you know who the players are and what they want. If you do not, every update feels like walking into a film halfway through.

You are not alone in feeling that. The Reuters Institute's [Digital News Report 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary) found that under-35s across countries are much more likely than older people to say the news is too hard to follow or understand. It also found that 40% of people sometimes or often avoid the news, up from 29% in 2017, and it names wars and volatile politics among the issues that wear people down.

Wars and crises are also upsetting. If that is what keeps you away, our post on [following the news without the anxiety](/blog/follow-the-news-without-the-anxiety) is a good place to start. This post is about making it make sense.

## What is geopolitics, in plain words?

Geopolitics is how geography, power and money shape what countries do. A country's location, its resources, what it has to import and who it depends on all push its choices. Leaders matter, but they work inside those limits.

Once you see it that way, most stories get simpler. You stop asking "what did they say today?" and start asking "what do they need, and what is stopping them?"

## Why should someone in India care?

Because it reaches your wallet faster than you might expect. Two examples, both from primary sources.

**Oil and the Strait of Hormuz.** The US Energy Information Administration [reports](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=65504) that in 2024 about 20 million barrels of oil a day moved through the Strait of Hormuz, roughly 20% of global petroleum liquids consumption. It estimates that 84% of the crude oil and condensate going through the strait went to Asian markets, and that China, India, Japan and South Korea together took 69%. India also imports most of its crude oil. Government data reported by [Rau's IAS Compass](https://compass.rauias.com/current-affairs/india-rising-crude-oil-import-dependency/) put import dependence at 88.2% for April to February of 2024-25, and a CII and EY study reported by [OilPrice.com](https://oilprice.com/Latest-Energy-News/World-News/India-Remains-Dependent-on-Imports-for-90-of-Oil-Demand.amp.html) says about 90% of crude demand is met by imports. So tension in the Gulf is not distant news. It can show up in fuel prices, inflation and the rupee.

**Shipping and the Red Sea.** In January 2024, UNCTAD [said](https://unctad.org/press-material/unctad-raises-alarms-escalating-disruptions-global-trade-due-geopolitical-tensions) that weekly transits through the Suez Canal had fallen 42% over the previous two months, as attacks in the Red Sea pushed ships onto longer routes. The same release notes that sea transport carries over 80% of the world's goods. Longer routes mean higher freight costs and slower deliveries for exporters and importers alike.

You do not need to memorise these numbers. The point is the habit: when you see a story about a faraway place, ask what flows through it.

## What questions should you ask about any world story?

Use the same four questions every time. They turn a confusing headline into something you can explain in a sentence.

| Question | What it helps you see | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who wants what? | The goals of each side, stated and unstated | A country wants secure energy supply and good terms with several partners |
| What does geography allow? | Borders, sea routes, chokepoints, resources | A narrow strait that most Gulf oil must pass through |
| Where is the money? | Trade, energy, debt, sanctions, investment | Who buys, who sells and who pays more if routes close |
| What usually happens next? | The pattern from past episodes | Earlier crises in the same place and how they ended |

The fourth question is the one that saves you the most stress. Many scary headlines describe a risk, not an outcome. Looking at how similar moments played out before keeps you calm and closer to the truth.

## How much time does it take?

Less than an hour a week. Here is a simple routine. It is a suggestion, not a research-backed formula, so adjust it to your week.

- **Most days, about five minutes.** Scan the headlines from one source that ends, and note anything in your anchor regions. Our guide to [staying informed in 5 minutes a day](/blog/stay-informed-in-5-minutes-a-day) covers this part.
- **Once a week, about 30 to 40 minutes.** One longer read, podcast or briefing on the biggest world story of the week. This is where understanding actually builds.
- **When something new appears, one background piece.** If a country or conflict you know nothing about keeps showing up, read one good overview before reading more updates. Updates without background are what make geopolitics feel like noise.

## How do you choose your anchor regions?

Do not try to follow the whole world. Choose two or three places that affect your work, money or family, and follow those well. For many readers in India, a sensible start is:

1. **The Gulf and West Asia,** because of oil, shipping and the many Indians who work there.
2. **China,** because of trade, technology supply chains and the border.
3. **The United States,** because of trade policy, tech, markets and the dollar.

If you work in a specific sector, swap one of these for what matters to you. Someone in shipping might add the Red Sea. Someone in pharma might add Europe. Everything else you can follow only when it becomes a big story.

## Where can you get reliable geopolitics news without the noise?

Mix one fast source with one slow one. Here are the main options and their trade-offs.

- **Official statements.** India's [Ministry of External Affairs](https://www.mea.gov.in/) publishes press releases and briefings. They are the primary record of what India has said, though naturally they present the government's view.
- **Wire services and major newspapers.** Good for what happened and when. Less space for why.
- **Think tanks and trackers.** The Council on Foreign Relations runs a [Global Conflict Tracker](https://www.cfr.org/global-conflict-tracker) that groups conflicts by region and status, with a background page for each. Indian think tanks such as the [Observer Research Foundation](https://www.orfonline.org/) publish analysis from an Indian angle. Every think tank has its own outlook, so read more than one.
- **Weekly podcasts and newsletters.** Good for the weekly slot. Pick one that cites its sources and is willing to say "we do not know yet".
- **Short video summaries.** Quick and easy to take in, with quality that varies a lot. Check who made it and whether they show their sources.

We make Qrio, a free Android app of short videos on business, startups and the world, so we have an interest in that last option. The advice holds whichever you choose. If you also want to keep up with markets and companies, we wrote a guide to [following Indian business news without the noise](/blog/best-ways-to-follow-indian-business-news).

## What should you avoid?

A few habits make geopolitics feel bigger and scarier than it is.

- **Live blogs and minute-by-minute feeds.** They reward refreshing, not understanding. Check back once at the end of the day instead.
- **Viral maps and threads.** They are often old, cropped or simply wrong. Treat them as a lead and confirm them elsewhere.
- **Single-source claims.** If only one account is saying it, wait.
- **Hot takes that predict doom.** Commentators who are always certain are often wrong. Prefer people who explain what they do not know.
- **Endless scrolling for updates.** If one story keeps pulling you back into a feed, see our tips on [how to stop doomscrolling](/blog/how-to-stop-doomscrolling).

One last note. Distressing world events can weigh on anyone. If worry about wars or crises is hurting your sleep, work or relationships for weeks, talk to a doctor or a counsellor.
