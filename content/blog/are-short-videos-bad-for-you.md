**The short answer:** maybe, but nobody can say for certain yet. The largest review so far links heavy short-video use to weaker attention, more anxiety and more stress. It also says plainly that most of the underlying studies cannot show that the videos cause those problems.

So this is not a case for panic. It is a case for paying attention to how you watch, not only how long. Here is what the research shows, how strong it is, and what you can sensibly do with it.

## What does the biggest study say?

The most cited piece of evidence is a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in the journal *Psychological Bulletin*, led by Lan Nguyen at Griffith University. [PsyPost's report on it](https://www.psypost.org/large-meta-analysis-links-tiktok-and-instagram-reels-to-poorer-cognitive-and-mental-health/) describes the scale: 71 studies in the review, 70 in the statistical analysis, and 98,299 participants in total.

The main findings, as reported:

- **Thinking and focus:** heavier short-video use had a moderate link to poorer cognitive performance. Attention and inhibitory control, which is your ability to stop yourself doing the easy thing, showed the most consistent links.
- **Mood:** a small but significant link to more anxiety, depression and stress. Sleep problems were also linked to use.
- **Body image and self-esteem:** no significant overall link. Results varied from study to study.

A "moderate" link is real but not dramatic. It describes a pattern across many people, not what will happen to you.

## Does how long you watch matter?

Less than you might think. The review found that studies using addiction-style scales reported stronger negative links than studies that only counted minutes. In plain words, the sense that you cannot stop seems to matter more than the number on your screen-time report.

That fits what we wrote about [why we doomscroll](/blog/why-we-doomscroll). Feeds are built so that the next video arrives before you decide to leave. Compulsive use is a design outcome, not only a personal failing.

It also gives you a better test than a daily time limit. Ask yourself: did I choose to start, and could I stop if I wanted to? If the answer to the second question is no, that is the signal worth noticing.

## Why can't the research say short videos cause harm?

This is the part headlines skip. The same reporting lists the main limits of the review:

- **Most studies were cross-sectional.** They measured people once, so they cannot show which came first.
- **Reverse cause is possible.** People who are already anxious, low or distracted may turn to short videos as a way to cope or escape.
- **Usage was often self-reported.** People are not good at estimating their own screen time.
- **The format is hard to isolate.** Few studies separated short videos from social media use in general.
- **Most research came from Asia,** with less from North America and Europe, and English-language papers dominate, so some work may have been missed.

The authors reportedly call for long-term studies that follow people over time, to test whether cutting back actually improves their health.

## Is there any experimental evidence?

A little. A separate systematic review by [Arouch and colleagues](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/short-form-video-cognitive-and-mental-health-outcomes-a-systematic-review/) pulled together 17 studies. Heavier use was linked to fragmented attention, weaker executive control and trouble managing emotions. Problematic, compulsive use was linked to anxiety and depressive symptoms, though results varied and were often specific to one platform.

Two details from that review are useful. First, some experimental studies found brief mood improvements, so short videos are not simply bad in the moment. Second, the authors say the relationship may run both ways: existing vulnerabilities may drive heavier use, and heavy use may deepen them. They also call for more longitudinal and experimental work.

The closest thing to a real test of cause is a [2022 randomised controlled trial](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/taking-a-one-week-break-from-social-media-improves-well-being-dep/) in *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking*. It put 154 adults, average age 29.6, into two groups. One group stopped using Facebook, Instagram, Twitter and TikTok for a week. The other carried on as usual.

After the week, the break group reported higher well-being and lower depression and anxiety. Less time on Twitter and TikTok partly explained the improvements in depression and anxiety, and TikTok alone accounted for the anxiety effect.

It is encouraging, but small and short. It covered four apps, not only short video, and it was a one-week window. It does not tell us what happens over months.

## What does the evidence look like at a glance?

| Question | What research suggests | How sure are we |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heavy use and weaker attention | Moderate link across 70 studies | Link is consistent, cause is unproven |
| Heavy use and anxiety, stress, depression | Small but significant link | Link is consistent, cause is unproven |
| Compulsive use vs minutes watched | Compulsive use shows stronger links | Fairly consistent across studies |
| Body image and self-esteem | No clear overall link | Mixed results |
| A one-week break from social apps | Better well-being, less anxiety and depression | One small trial, several apps |

## So what should you actually do?

You do not need to delete anything. You can use the evidence to make small, sensible changes.

1. **Watch the compulsion, not the clock.** If you open the app without deciding to, or find it hard to stop, treat that as the warning sign.
2. **Give your feed an ending.** A format with a natural end, such as a fixed set of videos for the day, takes away the "one more" pull. We wrote about this in [how to stop doomscrolling](/blog/how-to-stop-doomscrolling).
3. **Protect your nights.** The review linked use to sleep problems. Our guide to [doomscrolling at night](/blog/doomscrolling-at-night) has simple ways to break the bedtime habit.
4. **Try your own one-week test.** The trial above is small, but you can run a private version. Take a week off and notice your mood and focus. It is your data, not a study.
5. **Mind the content, not only the format.** The mixed findings on body image suggest that what you watch may matter as much as the fact that it is short.

If scrolling is tied to low mood, constant anxiety or trouble sleeping, it is worth talking to a doctor or a counsellor. A video habit is rarely the whole story.

## Are all short videos the same?

No, and the research does not claim they are. The studies measure a format, not a quality. A feed designed to keep you looping is a different experience from a short set of videos that explain one idea and then stop.
